How it works

live perturbation

A body is 60% water

Water absorbs 2.4 and 5 GHz. Walking through a room shifts RSSI by one to three dBm, and it shifts each access point by a different amount depending on what's between it and the receiver. That difference is the signal. Proximity reads what the house was already broadcasting.

Inside Proximity

01

01 · Baseline

Scan an empty house for 30 seconds; a stable mean and spread for every BSSID.

02

02 · Perturbation

Every live reading becomes a z-score against that baseline. The deviation is the motion signal; raw dBm never is.

03

03 · Zone inference

Each room has a signature: which BSSIDs deviate, and in which direction. Live deviations are matched against the library; past 2.5σ the zone trips.

04

04 · Track

Zone transitions over time become a path. Room-level resolution, no better, and the code doesn't pretend otherwise.

the rewrite

The first architecture solved the wrong problem: it fingerprinted the scanner's own position (which room is the laptop in?), a solved and useless question when the laptop never moves. The corrected model keeps the scanner fixed and detects someone else by how their body perturbs the ambient field.

bugs that cost real time

An asyncio.Lock() built outside the event loop, a deprecated startup hook, and (the dumbest) a Windows launcher that died on a folder path with spaces and a trailing backslash. Windows scans go through netsh, which caps the poll rate and quietly caches; that ceiling, not the physics, limits time resolution.

honest scope

Presence and motion are reliable; room-level zoning works after calibration. Sub-room position, counting people, and seeing through more than a couple of walls without a second access point don't. The simulator's physics are simplified; the method is the real one. The upgrade paths are CSI (sensitive enough for breathing) and 802.11mc round-trip timing.

Ambient RF is already everywhere in the house, already carrying the information.
